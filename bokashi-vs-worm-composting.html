Bokashi vs. Worm Composting

Compare bokashi fermentation and worm composting by space, accepted scraps, effort, outputs, and what happens after processing.

By Newell Davis, World Composting. More than 30 years of composting experience, with practical testing across multiple composting methods. About the author.

Bokashi and worm composting both help divert kitchen scraps from the trash, but they work in different ways. Bokashi ferments food waste in an airtight bucket. Worm composting uses worms and microorganisms to turn suitable food scraps into castings. The better choice depends on your space, the scraps you want to process, and what you can do with the finished material.

FactorBokashiWorm composting
How it worksAnaerobic fermentation in a sealed bucketWorms and microbes consume and process organic material
SpaceCompact indoor bucket; needs a place to finish fermented scrapsSmall indoor or sheltered outdoor bin; needs bedding and ventilation
Food scrapsCan handle a wider range of cooked foods, including some meat and dairy, when managed correctlyBest for plant-based scraps; avoid meat, dairy, oily and salty foods
OutputFermented pre-compost that still needs burial or further compostingFinished worm castings and partially processed bedding
EffortAdd scraps, inoculant, compress, seal, and drain liquidMaintain bedding moisture, feed modestly, and monitor conditions
Best fitSmall spaces and households wanting to process a broader range of food wasteGardeners who want castings and enjoy maintaining a living compost system

Which should you choose?

Choose bokashi if you need a compact way to ferment a broad mix of kitchen scraps and have somewhere to bury or further compost the result. Choose worms if you mainly have plant-based scraps and want to produce worm castings. Some households use both: bokashi for scraps that are unsuitable for a worm bin, then finished bokashi pre-compost is buried or added to a separate composting system.
