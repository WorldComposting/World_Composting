BioChar Pyrolysis Science: How Heat Creates Soil Carbon

Understand the science behind biochar production — pyrolysis temperatures, oxygen exclusion, and how heat transforms wood into stable soil carbon.

What Is Pyrolysis?

Pyrolysis is the thermal decomposition of organic material in the absence of oxygen. When wood or plant matter is heated to high temperatures (typically 300-700°C) without air, it breaks down into three products: biochar (solid carbon), bio-oil (liquid), and syngas (combustible gas).

The key difference between pyrolysis and normal combustion is oxygen exclusion. Without oxygen, the material cannot burn to ash — instead, it transforms into a stable form of carbon that resists decomposition for hundreds to thousands of years.

Temperature Zones and Their Effects

  • Low temperature (300-400°C): Produces more bio-oil, less char. Good for fuel production but lower-quality soil amendment.
  • Medium temperature (400-550°C): Optimal range for agricultural biochar. Balanced porosity and carbon stability.
  • High temperature (550-700°C): Produces dense, stable char with fewer volatile compounds. Best for long-term soil carbon storage.

Why Oxygen Must Be Excluded

If oxygen reaches the biomass during heating, it will combust into CO₂ and ash instead of forming biochar. This is why proper kiln design — whether a simple trench, a drum retort, or an industrial reactor — must create an oxygen-starved environment.

Carbon Stability in Soil

The carbon bonds formed during pyrolysis are extremely stable. Unlike regular organic matter that decomposes in months, biochar can persist in soil for 100-1,000+ years. This makes it one of the most effective tools for long-term carbon sequestration.

Home Production Tips

  • Use a metal drum with small air holes near the bottom for controlled oxygen intake during ignition, then seal it.
  • Aim for 400-550°C — the char should be black and brittle, not smoky or oily.
  • Cool in water or buried soil to prevent re-oxidation.
