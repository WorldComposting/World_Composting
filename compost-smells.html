Why Does My Compost Smell?

Diagnose sour, rotten, ammonia, and sulfur smells in worm bins, compost piles, and bokashi buckets with practical fixes.

By Newell Davis, World Composting. More than 30 years of composting experience, with hands-on testing across worm bins, hot composting, and other systems. About the author.

Healthy finished compost should smell earthy. A sour, rotten, ammonia, or sulfur smell is a useful warning that conditions inside the system need attention. The right fix depends on the smell and the composting method.

Sour or rotten smell

This usually points to excess moisture, compacted material, or too much fresh nitrogen-rich food without enough air and carbon. In a pile or tumbler, mix in dry leaves, shredded cardboard, or untreated paper and turn the material to restore airflow. In a worm bin, pause feeding, remove any large pocket of rotting food, and mix in dry shredded cardboard. Check again after a day or two.

Ammonia smell

A sharp ammonia smell often means nitrogen-rich material is out of balance with carbon. Add dry browns such as dry leaves, shredded cardboard, or straw. Mix them through the pile rather than leaving a layer on top. Avoid adding more grass clippings or food scraps until the smell clears.

Rotten-egg or sulfur smell

A strong sulfur smell can indicate oxygen-starved, waterlogged material. Drain excess liquid where appropriate, loosen compacted material, and add dry carbon. For bokashi, a sour, pickled smell is expected; a putrid or rotten-egg smell suggests excess liquid, poor sealing, or contamination. Drain the bucket and check the lid and seal.

Prevention

Balance wet greens with dry browns, avoid packing material tightly, keep moisture appropriate for the method, and add food in amounts the system can process. Worm bins should be fed based on how quickly previous scraps disappear, not on a fixed schedule.
