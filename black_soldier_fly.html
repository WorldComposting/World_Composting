What is Black Soldier Fly Composting?

Black soldier fly composting uses the voracious larvae of Hermetia illucens to convert organic waste into fertilizer and high-protein animal feed in as little as 3-4 weeks. Unlike worms, black soldier fly larvae happily eat meat, dairy, cooked food, and citrus — the scraps most home systems cannot handle. They work best in warm conditions (86–95°F), prefer drier material than worms, and self-harvest: when they are ready to pupate, they climb out of the bin on their own, leaving behind nutrient-rich frass.

Why Choose Black Soldier Flies?

  • Eats What Worms Can't — Meat, fish, dairy, cooked food, and citrus are all fair game. This makes black soldier flies the ideal system for households that want to compost 100% of their kitchen waste without sorting around animal products.
  • Self-Harvesting — Ready larvae crawl out of the bin on a ramp when it is time to pupate. No sifting, no digging through bedding: you simply collect them from a tray each week.
  • Two Valuable Outputs — You get frass (larval excrement) that works as an excellent garden fertilizer, plus larvae and pupae that are about 40% protein by dry weight and a favorite feed for chickens, fish, ducks, and birds.
  • Low Odor — A healthy black soldier fly bin smells mild, like fermenting compost. The larvae consume waste faster than it can rot, which keeps odors far below those of an unmanaged pile.
Troubleshooting

Black soldier fly females lay on decomposing matter, not fresh food:

  • Pre-ferment your feedstock — Let scraps break down for 5–7 days before exposing them to the outdoors. Fresh food will not attract egg-laying females.
  • Keep it warm — Flies are most active above 60°F, and wild populations peak from late spring through fall. A cold bin in early spring may simply need patience.
  • Start with purchased larvae — If you are starting from scratch, buying a few hundred live larvae is the fastest route to a working system while you wait for wild flies to find you.

Sluggish larvae usually point to one of three problems:

  • Temperature — Below about 50°F larvae slow down dramatically. Move the bin to a warmer spot or insulate it.
  • Moisture — Too wet means drowning risk; add dry browns like shredded cardboard until the pile stops dripping.
  • Food availability — Add fresh chopped scraps. Persistent mold on feedstock can also suppress feeding, so remove any affected material.

Mass die-off or mass escape usually means the bin conditions have gone wrong:

  • Too wet — The most common cause. Add dry browns and improve ventilation until the pile feels damp, not dripping.
  • Too cold — Larvae cannot survive freezing temperatures; move the bin indoors or into a sheltered space.
  • Overcrowding — If you have more larvae than feedstock can support, split the colony into a second bin and keep feeding both.
