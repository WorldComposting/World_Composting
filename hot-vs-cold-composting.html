Hot vs. Cold Composting

Compare hot and cold composting methods, including speed, effort, temperature, materials, weed seeds, and when to choose each.

By Newell Davis, World Composting. More than 30 years of composting experience, with practical testing across multiple composting methods. About the author.

Hot and cold composting both turn organic material into compost, but they rely on different levels of microbial activity and management. Hot composting aims to build and maintain thermophilic temperatures. Cold composting is slower and usually requires less turning and monitoring.

FactorHot compostingCold composting
SpeedFaster when the pile is large, balanced, moist, and aeratedSlower; material may take many months or longer
ManagementNeeds attention to pile size, moisture, carbon-nitrogen balance, and turningLower effort; add material and allow it to break down
TemperatureDesigned to reach thermophilic temperatures; monitor with a compost thermometerUsually stays near ambient temperature with modest biological warming
MaterialsBest with a balanced mix of greens and browns in sufficient volumeWorks with mixed yard trimmings and plant scraps added over time
Weed seeds and pathogensA properly managed hot pile can reduce many weed seeds and pathogens when all material receives adequate heatDo not rely on cold composting to sanitize weed seeds or diseased material
Best fitPeople who want a faster process and can manage a pilePeople who prefer a simple, low-maintenance approach

Which should you choose?

Choose hot composting when you have enough material to build a substantial pile and are willing to monitor and turn it. Choose cold composting when simplicity matters more than speed. Neither method guarantees a particular result: pile size, ingredients, moisture, aeration, and how consistently conditions are maintained all matter.
